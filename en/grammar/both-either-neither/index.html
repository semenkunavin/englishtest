Both, Either and Neither: Easy A2 Grammar Guide

We use both, either and neither to talk about two people or two things. Both means the two together, either means one of the two, and neither means not one and not the other. These words are very common in everyday English.

  1. Use both to talk about two things together: both + plural noun, e.g. both hands. Both of + the/my/these is also correct: both of my sisters.
  2. Use either to mean one of two things: either + singular noun, e.g. either way. Either of + the/my + plural noun: either of the answers.
  3. Neither means not one and not the other. Neither + singular noun: neither boy. Neither of + the/my + plural noun: neither of my friends.
  4. Both ... and ... connects two things: Both Tom and Anna are here.
  5. Either ... or ... and neither ... nor ... join two options: Either tea or coffee; Neither tea nor coffee.
  6. After neither (as a subject) use a singular verb in careful English: Neither is correct. But many speakers also say Neither are correct.

Examples

  • I like both books.I like both books.
  • You can take either bus.You can take either bus.
  • Neither answer is right.Neither answer is right.
  • Both of my brothers play football.Both of my brothers play football.
  • I don't want either of the shirts.I don't want either of the shirts.
  • Neither of the shops is open today.Neither of the shops is open today.

Typical mistakes

Both of my brother is a teacher.
Both of my brothers are teachers.
Both refers to two things, so use a plural noun and a plural verb.
I don't like neither of them.
I don't like either of them.
Use either after a negative verb. Neither is already negative.
Neither of the boys are here.
Neither of the boys is here.
In careful English neither of takes a singular verb: is.
